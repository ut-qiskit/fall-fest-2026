Start here

Setup is three commands. Use the first set on macOS or Linux, the second on Windows.

Download and unzip

Keep the whole folder together. The notebooks look for the python directory beside them.

Create the environment

Run these from inside the extracted folder.

# macOS or Linux
python3.12 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/python -m pip install -r python/requirements.txt

# Windows PowerShell
py -3.12 -m venv .venv
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe -m pip install -r python/requirements.txt

Check it works

This runs a short 64-shot simulation. If it passes, you are set up.

.venv/bin/python python/check_environment.py

Open the notebooks

Work through them in order. Pick a kernel from the environment you just made.

.venv/bin/python -m jupyterlab
Stuck on setup? Bring your laptop to the Friday session, or email sabiqislam@utexas.edu. Do not spend your whole Monday fighting an install.

What you are building

A circuit often has more qubits than the machine available, or gets too deep once routing is added, or loses accuracy to uneven noise. Circuit cutting is one answer: split the circuit, run the pieces separately, and reconstruct the result classically.

It is not free. Cutting multiplies the number of experiments you have to run, and your shot budget does not grow to match. So the interesting question is not whether cutting works, it is when it is worth it. You will measure that on cases where it helps and cases where it does not.

Every shot counts against one allowance. Twelve experiments at 1,000 shots each costs 12,000 shots, whether you cut or not. Each case here gives you 24,000.

You will compare two ways of running a circuit, plain hardware-aware transpilation and Qiskit's automatic cut finder, and two ways of spending the budget across the resulting experiments. Four combinations, ten cases, one number at the end.

Everyone runs the same benchmark

So the results mean something next to each other. One seed, one shot budget, the same ten cases for every team.

Generate the suite

Use this seed exactly. It fixes the circuits, the graphs, the angles, and the noise profiles, so your ten cases are identical to everyone else's. 

python python/generate_benchmarks.py --split public --preset smoke \
  --master-seed 20261116 --output data/fallfest

Run the comparison

Two execution modes and two ways of spending the shot budget. This is the experiment: four combinations, same circuits, same 24,000 shots per case. 

python python/evaluate_planners.py --manifest data/fallfest/manifest.json \
  --modes direct,auto_cut --allocations equal,coefficient \
  --replicates 3 --output results/fallfest

Score it

This prints macro RMSE and feasibility coverage. Those two numbers are what you report, and what we can line up across teams. 

python python/score_results.py --input results/fallfest/evaluation.json

Draw one plan

Exports the actual cuts, fragments, and shot ledger as an SVG. Include it in your writeup. Case 1 is width-constrained, so you will see real cuts; try case 0 as well, where the planner decides not to cut at all. 

python python/visualize_plan.py --manifest data/fallfest/manifest.json \
  --case 1 --mode auto_cut --output results/fallfest-plan
Four of the ten cases are ones where the circuit already fits and cutting is the wrong call. Two more are controls with an exact known answer. Those are there on purpose: finding where cutting hurts is as much of a result as finding where it helps.

Check your numbers against these

Step 2 takes about a minute and writes 120 run records. Step 3 should print four methods. If your table looks like this, your setup is correct and you can start the actual work.

Mode and allocationCoverageMacro RMSEMeaning
direct, equal0.60noneCannot run 4 of 10 cases, so it does not qualify.
direct, coefficient0.60noneSame. Allocation cannot fix a width limit.
auto_cut, equal1.000.0322Covers everything, but spends the budget bluntly.
auto_cut, coefficient1.000.0258Same circuits, same shots, 20 percent less error.

Sampling is random, so your last digits will differ. The ordering should not.

Look closely at the first two rows before you conclude cutting wins. On the six cases direct execution can run, its error is about 0.0267, better than cut execution managed across all ten. Cutting bought you coverage, not accuracy. Explaining that trade is the heart of this challenge.

Then make it yours

The commands above give everyone the same baseline. Your submission is what you do past that point.

The clearest thing to investigate is the shot allocation. You have one fixed budget. Spending it evenly across every subexperiment is one choice; weighting it by how much each subexperiment contributes is another. Run both, look at the error, and explain the difference.

From there you can change one condition at a time and see what moves: 

python python/generate_matched_conditions.py \
  --manifest data/fallfest/manifest.json \
  --axis topology --output data/fallfest-topology

The axis can be topology, noise, width, or shots.

Questions worth answering:

  • On which cases did cutting beat running directly, and by how much?
  • Where did it lose, and why would you expect that?
  • What did the cut cost you in shots per useful measurement?
  • How wide is your uncertainty, and does it cover the ideal answer?
  • What happens to all of this when the topology or noise changes?

The four workload families

A generator builds fresh instances of each from a random seed, so there is no dataset to download.

FamilyWhat it isWhat you estimate
QAOA / MaxCut Alternating graph-interaction and mixing layers. A weighted ZZ correlation. You are not looking for a better graph cut; the graph and angles are given.
Spin dynamics A product-formula circuit for an Ising model with fields. Transverse magnetization of the circuit as written.
Variational energy A fixed trial circuit and a normalized Ising Hamiltonian. Its energy. One estimation step, not a full variational loop.
Controlled modular Small circuit modules joined by controlled rotations. An endpoint correlation. "Modular" means circuit modules, not modular arithmetic.

How it is judged

Everyone runs the same four commands, so those numbers alone will not separate you. What you did with them will.

CriterionWeightWhat we look for
The comparison30%Direct against cut, equal against coefficient allocation, on the shared cases. Reported with the failures left in.
The explanation30%Why the numbers came out that way. The width, depth, and sampling trade-off in your own words, tied to your own results.
Correctness20%Reconstruction done right, shots accounted for honestly, uncertainty stated and not understated.
Going further10%Anything past the baseline: a changed condition, an extra axis, a hypothesis you tested.
Reproducibility10%We can run your notebook start to finish and get your numbers.
A case that fails stays in your results. Hiding it costs you more than the failure does. A submission that shows cutting losing on four cases and explains why beats one that reports a single number with no context.

What to submit

By Thursday November 19, 11:59 pm. Winners announced Friday at the IBM Quantum talk, PMA 9.222, 2 pm.

  1. Your notebook, runnable start to finish.
  2. The scores from the shared suite: macro RMSE and coverage.
  3. Your direct against cut and equal against coefficient comparison.
  4. Complete results, including the cases that failed.
  5. One execution-plan SVG.
  6. A write-up, roughly one to two pages, of what you found and why.

Submit by emailing a repository link or a zip to sabiqislam@utexas.edu with your team name.

Reading

The first three are enough to start. The rest when you need them.

  • Qiskit cutting explanation - gate versus wire cuts, local operations, sampling overhead.
  • Gate cutting to reduce circuit width - partitioning, experiment generation, reconstruction.
  • Automatic cut finding - the baseline you are compared against.
  • Multiple observables - weighted Pauli reconstruction.
  • Qiskit bit ordering - for when your diagram order and your bitstring order disagree.
  • Aer noise models - gate versus readout error.
  • Peng et al., Mitarai and Fujii, Schmitt et al. - the papers behind gate cutting. Optional.
